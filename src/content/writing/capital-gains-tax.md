## Introduction

Capital Gains Tax (CGT) is a levy on the profit made from the sale of assets such as property, stocks, and businesses. With ongoing discussions about raising the CGT in the UK, many are debating the potential consequences of such a move. While an increase could generate significant government revenue and contribute to wealth redistribution, it may have negative impacts on investment, entrepreneurial activity, and even the creative industries.

In the last weeks, The Guardian reported an increase in capital gains tax to the region of 33–39%. Since then, Keir Starmer has come out and rubbished that statement and declined to speak on it any further. As of right now, the basic rate of capital gains on property is 18% but for higher earners, it's 24%. For other assets, i.e. shares, the basic rate is 10% and the rate for higher earners is 20%.

This has sparked conversation, but why? In this article, we will explore both the positive and negative impacts of increasing CGT, particularly focusing on the effects it could have on London's high-net-worth individuals (the so-called "1%") as well as how it might deter innovation and entrepreneurial spirit.

## Positive impacts of raising Capital Gains Tax

One of the most significant benefits of increasing Capital Gains Tax is the potential for higher tax revenue. The government could use this revenue to fund essential public services such as education, healthcare and infrastructure or to reduce the national debt. Given the current economic challenges facing the UK, this injection of capital could help improve the country's financial standing while addressing urgent public needs.

In addition to boosting government revenue, a higher CGT could also play a role in wealth redistribution. By taxing investment returns more heavily, those who benefit the most from rising asset values (typically the wealthiest individuals) would contribute more to the public purse, reducing wealth inequality in the UK and ensuring a fairer economic system that benefits more people.

Furthermore, some economists argue that higher CGT could discourage speculative investment, especially in sectors like housing, which has contributed to inflated property prices in cities like London. By discouraging speculative buying, it may lead to more affordable housing and create a healthier, more stable economy.

## Negative impacts of raising Capital Gains Tax

However, raising CGT could also have negative consequences. One of the most significant drawbacks is the potential reduction in investment incentives. Investors may become more hesitant to put their money into assets like stocks or property, fearing that higher taxes will erode their returns. This could slow down economic growth, as investment is a crucial driver of expansion in key sectors, including technology, infrastructure, and property development.

High-net-worth individuals, particularly those residing in London, may consider moving their wealth elsewhere to avoid the higher tax burden. This capital flight could shrink the UK's tax base, reducing overall tax revenues in the long run.

For London, which is home to a significant number of the country's wealthiest residents, a rise in CGT could lead to a reduction in the city's global standing as a hub for finance and investment. The "1%" could relocate their investments to countries with more favourable tax regimes, causing a potential outflow of capital that impacts the broader economy.

Entrepreneurs could also be disproportionately affected by higher CGT. Starting and selling a business often involves significant risk, and if the potential rewards are reduced by higher taxes on the eventual sale of the business, some may be discouraged from taking that leap. This could lead to a decrease in new business ventures, stifling innovation and creativity across industries.

## Impact on the 1% and capital flight in London

London has long been a magnet for high-net-worth individuals, offering them a dynamic, global city with favourable conditions for investment. However, an increase in CGT could prompt some of these individuals to move their investments, or even their residences, to countries with lower tax rates. This capital flight could significantly affect the city's financial sector, which thrives on the presence of wealthy investors and entrepreneurs.

The "1%", those with substantial wealth tied up in investments, could be particularly affected by a CGT hike. While they have the means to move their money across borders, doing so could weaken London's economy, which benefits from the spending and investment activities of these wealthy individuals.

Moreover, if too many of these high-net-worth individuals relocate, it could reduce the government's ability to collect tax revenue from these sources, negating some of the intended benefits of raising CGT in the first place.

Beyond this, there's the potential for a shift in the type of capital that remains in the UK. If high-growth sectors like technology and start-ups see less investment due to tax concerns, it could reduce the city's ability to remain competitive on the global stage.

For London, the threat of losing its place as a premier financial hub becomes a pressing concern, especially in the wake of Brexit, which has already challenged the city's position in the global financial landscape.

## Deterring creativity and innovation

One of the more concerning consequences of raising CGT is its potential to deter creativity and innovation. Entrepreneurs and innovators, often at the forefront of technological advancement and creative industries, could see a higher CGT as a barrier to launching new ventures.

For many, the promise of financial reward at the end of a successful project is what drives them to take the risks involved in building something new, and if that reward is diminished by higher taxes, they may be less likely to take those risks.

This could have broader implications for the UK's creative and tech sectors, which rely heavily on innovation and entrepreneurship. Start-ups and small businesses, especially in areas like software development, media production and design, are essential for driving forward new ideas. With higher CGT, the potential returns on their hard work could be significantly reduced, leading to fewer start-ups and a slower pace of innovation.

Additionally, the rise in tax may discourage foreign entrepreneurs from bringing their ventures to the UK. The country's start-up ecosystem has benefited from international talent and capital, but if tax policies make it less attractive to innovate in the UK, other cities, such as Berlin or Dublin, could emerge as more favourable alternatives, further harming the UK's ability to remain competitive in the global race for innovation and creativity.

## Conclusion

Raising Capital Gains Tax in the UK could bring both benefits and challenges. On the one hand, it could generate significant tax revenue and promote wealth redistribution, addressing growing inequality.

On the other hand, it risks discouraging investment, driving capital out of the country, and deterring entrepreneurs from taking the risks necessary to create new businesses and innovate.

For London, the potential for capital flight and a weakened entrepreneurial environment is a real concern. The city's status as a global financial hub could be at stake if the wealthiest residents decide to relocate their investments.

Policymakers will need to carefully weigh these positive and negative impacts, ensuring that any changes to the CGT regime strike a balance between encouraging growth and promoting fairness within the economy.
